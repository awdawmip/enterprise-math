## 3. Complete finite-order proof (chat parts 0-4)

### 0. Formal coefficients faithfully describe actual operators

Suppose sum_(i=0)^N h^(i) A_i is zero for every h in R^2. Applying it to
each constant basis row gives A_0=0. Inductively, suppose A_0,...,A_(k-1)
are zero and apply the operator to r^k times either constant basis row.
Terms of order greater than k vanish; lower-order coefficient matrices
are already zero; the remaining term is k! times that row of A_k.
Thus A_k=0 since K has characteristic zero. This covers k=0,...,N.

The finite expansion is therefore unique as an operator on the ACTUAL
R^2, so order and leading coefficient are well defined. Only K[r] inside
R was used: no point evaluation, local fundamental solution, new field
or pointwise invertibility of nabla was assumed.

The Leibniz rule and dR/dr contained in R show that composition remains
in A_fin. In particular L^N has its coefficients in the same R and its
highest derivative term is h^(N) I. No scientific program computes it.

### 1. The leading coefficient of a commuting operator is scalar

Let nonzero T in Z_fin(L) have order N, with A_N nonzero. In
(L composed T - T composed L)(h), the h^(N+1) terms cancel. Terms from
A_(N-1) cancel as well when N is positive. The coefficient of h^(N) is
exactly

    A_N' + A_N D - D A_N.

Indeed, L applied to T contributes A_N'+A_ND at that derivative order,
while T applied to hD contributes D A_N there. Faithfulness from part0
allows coefficient comparison. Thus commutation forces

    A_N' = D A_N - A_N D.

This is EXACTLY the row-variance horizontal-End equation of input1.
That accepted same-R/D theorem gives A_N=c_N I for c_N in K. Since
A_N is nonzero, c_N is nonzero. We have not assumed an arbitrary
matrix leading coefficient invertible: it becomes a nonzero scalar
only after this legitimate application of input1.

For N=0 the same equation applies directly and already says T=c_0 I.

### 2. Induction covers all finite orders, including zero

For N positive, c_N is a d/dr constant in K. Hence c_N L^N commutes
with L and has exactly the same leading coefficient as T. Therefore

    T - c_N L^N

has strictly lower order and still commutes with L. If this difference
is zero the descent ends; otherwise apply the same argument again.
Since N was finite, the descent terminates and gives

    T = sum_(i=0)^N c_i L^i,       c_i in K.

The zero operator is the zero polynomial, and order0 was covered in
part1. Conversely any such K-coefficient polynomial commutes with L
and is a finite differential operator over the very same R. Both
containments in Z_fin(L)=K[L] follow; no positive finite order or
nonconstant matrix-coefficient possibility has been omitted.

### 3. Polynomial uniqueness and algebra structure

If nonzero f(t) in K[t] has degree n and leading coefficient f_n, then
f(L) has highest derivative term h^(n) f_n I, which is nonzero. Thus

    order(f(L)) = n,              f(L) != 0.

Consequently the K-algebra map K[t] -> Z_fin(L), f -> f(L), is injective.
Part2 proves surjectivity. It is an algebra map because constants in K
commute with L and multiplication is composition. This proves the
claimed K-algebra isomorphism, without an extra finite-order algebraic

### 4. Units and the finite-inverse boundary

Suppose T in Z_fin(L) has a two-sided inverse S in the ambient A_fin.
Starting from TL=LT, multiplication on the left and right by S yields
LS=SL. Thus the inverse itself lies in the same centralizer. Parts2-3
write T=f(L), S=g(L) and turn TS=I into

    f(t) g(t) = 1 in K[t].

Both polynomials are therefore nonzero constants. Conversely cI with
c in K^* has inverse c^(-1)I in A_fin and in the centralizer. Zero is
not a unit, and no positive-order polynomial has such a finite inverse.
This proves the unit statement both for ambient finite two-sided
invertibility and for units defined internally to Z_fin(L).
